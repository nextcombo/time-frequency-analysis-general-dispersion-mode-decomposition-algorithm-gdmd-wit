In the fields of non-destructive testing and hydroacoustics, it is common to encounter dispersion signals with frequency-varying characteristics, which typically have a group delay (GD) in the time-frequency domain and are composed of multiple modes superimposed. This presents challenges for existing signal processing methods. This paper proposes a generalized dispersion mode decomposition (GDMD) method to precisely estimate dispersion modes and fully separate overlapping modes of dispersion signals. Specifically, a broadband dispersion signal model is first defined in the frequency domain. Based on this model, the mode decomposition problem is transformed into an optimal dispersion compensation problem, where the method simultaneously searches for the optimal mode and its GD, thereby completely eliminating the dispersion effects of all modes. Furthermore, based on the output results of GDMD, high-quality TF distributions can be constructed to clearly reveal the TF patterns of multimodal dispersion signals. Simulation examples and practical applications such as wheel fault diagnosis and Lamb wave analysis demonstrate the effectiveness of GDMD.
1. Introduction: Dispersion signals have been extensively studied in various fields, such as structural health monitoring [1, 2], non-destructive testing [3-5], underwater acoustics [6, 7], and biomedical applications [8, 9]. Dispersion signals exhibit frequency-dependent characteristics, where group delay (GD) is defined as the derivative of the phase function with respect to frequency, which is the most important parameter [10, 11]. GD contains valuable information sources and systems, thus it can be used for source localization and system identification. However, when broadband signals pass through nonlinear systems, different time delays will be generated at each frequency, leading to frequency-varying GD. Moreover, actual dispersion signals like guided waves often contain multiple signal modes with cross-GD curves (also known as dispersion curves), making signal analysis difficult. Therefore, accurately separating these cross-dispersion modes and extracting their GD curves are crucial for practical applications.
Dispersion signals are a type of typical non-stationary signal, characterized by time-varying or frequency-varying characteristics. Time-frequency analysis is one of the effective methods for processing non-stationary signals because it can simultaneously represent the features of the signal in both the time domain and the frequency domain. Traditional time-frequency analysis methods such as Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) and Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) have wide applications in various fields, but these methods often suffer from poor energy concentration. Quadratic time-frequency analysis methods like Wigner-Ville distributions are known to be used for fine-grained concentration, but they often encounter cross terms with nonlinear and multimodal signals. To improve the readability and concentration of the time-frequency distribution (TFD), researchers have proposed a post-processing technique called Time-Frequency Redistribution (TFR)[13], which moves the TFR coefficients from their original positions to the local energy centroid to sharpen the TFD. Synchronous Compression Transform (SST) [14] as a special case of TFR, has attracted considerable attention not only for its ability to enhance the TFD but also for its reversibility in signal reconstruction. However, SST still generates ambiguous TFDs for rapidly changing signals due to its inherent zero-order approximation to the signal. To address this issue, demodulation techniques [15-17] or higher-order approximation operators [18,19] have been used to improve the rapid signal characterization capability of SST. On the other hand, SST only reassigns TFR coefficients along the frequency axis, so it is less capable of analyzing pulse-like signals or dispersion signals with transient behavior. To reduce the energy diffusion along the time axis of transient signals, some researchers further developed time-reassigned SST [20,21] and horizontal SST [22]. One major limitation of current SST-based methods is that they cannot distinguish closely or cross signal modes because the TFR coefficients of these modes almost overlap, making them unsuitable for accurate redistribution. Moreover, noise interference may change the local TFR energy centroid, directly affecting the results of SST.
The parametric TFA method is another popular TF approach known for its signal-dependent resolution. This method assumes that signals can be represented by a parametric model, and then formulates the TFA problem as a parameter estimation problem. When the defined model matches the signal well, the TF method can achieve optimal TF resolution. Yang et al. [23] proposed a generalized parametric TFA transform (GPTFT), which employs different models such as polynomials, splines, and Fourier series to represent the instantaneous frequency (IF) of signals. Additionally, to estimate the GD of dispersed signals, a frequency domain GPTFT was developed as a dual version of the original method [10]. However, when a set of parameters can only match one signal pattern, each pattern exhibits different TF patterns. Therefore, the GPTFT cannot fully represent multimodal signals in a single execution. One possible solution is to use image filtering techniques to assemble the TFD generated through multiple executions of GPTFT (relative to different patterns) to form multimodal TFD [11]. Similarly, in some specific applications of dispersion analysis, researchers adopt specific dispersion models to construct effective parametric TF methods, such as the dispersion-based STFT [24] and dispersion Radon transform [25]. Clearly, one disadvantage of parametric methods is that they cannot be applied to more general cases where predefined models do not match the considered signals.
The above methods mainly target the TF representation of signals. We are particularly interested in TF analysis methods based on signal decomposition, which can extract inherent patterns of signals to clearly reveal their TF patterns. Over the past few decades, researchers have developed many well-known signal decomposition methods, such as empirical mode decomposition (EMD), empirical wavelet transform, and variational mode decomposition (VMD), most of which are conducted under narrowband conditions [26, 27]. These methods often suffer from the issue of over-decomposition when dealing with wideband signals, such as FM signals. Recently, Chen et al. proposed several signal decomposition methods based on chirp models, such as Variational Nonlinear Chirp Mode Decomposition (VNCMD) [28] and Adaptive Chirp Mode Decomposition (ACMD) [29, 30], for analyzing multimodal wideband chirp signals. One promising advantage of this method is that it can accurately estimate the IF and instantaneous amplitudes close to or even crossing signal modes, thus enabling the construction of a quality TFD [31]. However, these methods are not applicable to dispersion signal analysis because the chirp model cannot represent dispersion effects. For dispersion signal decomposition, Dispersion Compensation Methods (DCM) [32, 33] have attracted great interest. By using DCM, dispersion signals with varying GD can be transformed into instantaneous pulses (with constant GD), which can then be separated by short time-rectangular windows. It is worth mentioning that methods based on SST have also been reported for dispersion signal separation [34]. However, whether DCM or SST-based methods can separate dispersion modes in the TF domain overlaps.
In summary, there still lacks a generalized signal decomposition method that can handle cross- or overlapping dispersion patterns. Based on the ACMD's advantages in separating broadband chirped modes, a Generalized Dispersion Mode Decomposition (GDMD) method is proposed. The main contributions of this paper include two aspects: 1) Considering the frequency-dependent dispersion characteristics, a generalized (nonparametric) frequency domain dispersion signal model is proposed; 2) Based on this model, an algorithm for joint estimation is introduced to precisely estimate the dispersion gradient of cross-dispersion patterns, achieving mode separation. In fact, the suggested GDMD can be regarded as the best DCM's best GD estimation, allowing all pattern dispersion effects to be completely eliminated from the estimation results. Moreover, unlike traditional DCMs, the joint estimation algorithm can well balance the energy of cross-patterns near the crossover points, thus yielding accurate mode reconstruction results.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. After briefly describing the linearly time-varying signal model, Section 2 introduces the ACMD. The proposed GDMD method is detailed in Section 3. Numerical examples are presented in Section 4, followed by the practical application of GDMD in Section 5. Section 6 concludes the paper.
